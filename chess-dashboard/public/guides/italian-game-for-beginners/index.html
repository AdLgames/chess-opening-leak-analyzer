The Italian Game for beginners: understand the first moves

The Italian is easier to remember when every move has a job. Start with the centre, bring the pieces into play, and understand what the bishop is actually pointing at.

What are the first moves of the Italian Game?

The Italian Game begins 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4. White puts a pawn in the centre, develops a knight with a threat against e5, then brings the king’s bishop to an active diagonal. Black’s moves are part of the definition: if Black answers 1.e4 with …c5, you are in a Sicilian instead.

Think of these moves as a conversation. The e-pawn claims space and opens lines for the bishop and queen. Nf3 develops while attacking e5. Black’s …Nc6 develops and defends that pawn. Bc4 then develops another piece and looks towards f7. The point is coordinated development, not a promise of an immediate winning attack.

Why does the bishop go to c4?

From c4 the bishop attacks along the diagonal towards d5, e6 and f7. In the starting Italian position, f7 is occupied by a black pawn and defended by the king. That makes it a square to notice. It does not mean White can simply capture it: a sacrifice needs a concrete continuation.

The bishop’s influence also depends on what moves onto the diagonal. A black pawn on d5 can block the bishop’s route, and exchanges can open or close it. Before repeating a remembered attack, check the current board. Ask which pieces attack the target, which pieces defend it, and what happens after a capture.

Choose a plan after Black’s reply

After 3…Bc5, a quiet plan is 4.d3, followed by sensible development and castling. The d-pawn supports e4, and White can later decide how to develop the queen’s knight or prepare d4. In some lines c3 helps support that break. Playing c3 is a preparation move, not a rule to follow regardless of Black’s threats.

After 3…Nf6, Black attacks e4. This is the Two Knights Defence. Our starter repertoire again uses 4.d3 to support the centre. The sharper 4.Ng5 is a different branch with concrete replies to learn. Do not mix moves from the quiet and sharp branches just because the positions look similar.

Before pushing a central pawn, ask whether your king is safe and your pieces are ready for open lines. Castling often improves king safety and brings the rook closer to the centre. It does not make every pawn break good; the next move still needs checking.

Three habits that get beginners into trouble

Attacking f7 with too few pieces. A vulnerable-looking square is a clue, not an evaluation. Count the attackers and defenders, then calculate the opponent’s forcing response. If you cannot explain what follows, finish development instead of sacrificing on instinct.

Playing a setup without looking at Black. The Italian has familiar plans, but your opponent can challenge the centre or attack a piece. Before your preferred move, check for checks, captures and direct threats. Respond to the board you have.

Learning only White’s moves. Remember the reply that makes your next move useful. A short line with both sides’ ideas is worth more than a long sequence that breaks as soon as the opponent deviates. Keep a separate note for a different branch rather than force it into the same story.

A short way to practise the Italian

Start with one decision: after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6, where should the f1 bishop develop in this repertoire, and what does it influence? Play Bc4 in the exercise below. Then close the hint and describe the idea in your own words.

Next, learn the difference between Black’s …Bc5 and …Nf6 replies. Practise the relevant response from the actual position, not only by reciting the line from move one. On a later day, try it without help. Our recall records keep hinted answers separate from answers found first time without help.

Finally, play a few moves from a studied position against Stockfish. This is a chance to notice whether you can still develop sensibly when the exact line ends. It is practice, not a rating test or evidence that the whole opening is mastered.
